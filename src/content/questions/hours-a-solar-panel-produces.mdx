A panel sold as "1 kW" does not produce a kilowatt. It produces a kilowatt at
noon in June with a clean surface and a clear sky, and something less than that
every other moment of its life. The useful way to count is to ask how many hours
of *full* output the year adds up to — take everything the panel made and divide
it by its rated power.

Seville's array adds up to about 1,650 kWh over the year; Hamburg's to about
870. Both panels are identical. All that separates them is latitude, cloud and
the length of a winter day.

/>

<MarginNote>
  Equivalent full-load hours = annual kWh ÷ rated kW. It is the same quantity as
  the capacity factor, expressed in hours instead of a percentage.
</MarginNote>

The seasonal shape matters more than the total. Seville's worst month still
beats Hamburg's best, and Hamburg's December is barely a rounding error —
fourteen kilowatt-hours across the whole month, roughly one hot bath a week.
Sizing a system on the annual figure alone quietly assumes you can borrow
July's sunshine in December, which is precisely the thing a rooftop cannot do.

### Method

Both series are for a south-facing array at 35° tilt with standard system
losses, expressed per kilowatt-peak installed so the two cities can be compared
directly. Equivalent full-load hours divide the annual total by the rated
power; because the ratings are 1 kWp here, kWh and hours come out numerically
the same.
