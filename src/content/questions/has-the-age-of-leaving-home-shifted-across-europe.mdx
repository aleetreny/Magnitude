Every country in Europe has a door, and each one opens at a different age. What
almost none of them have is a door that moves.

Finland's door stands at 21.3, Croatia's at 31.5. Between the two lies **10.2
years**, twenty times what the middle country has moved in five. Fifteen of the
twenty-eight have not moved more than half a year.

It was just as still before. Across those twenty-one years the median country's
whole range is 1.6 years, and Germany's is 0.4: every reading between 23.7 and
24.1.

The age of leaving home reads like a fact about a generation. It behaves like a
fact about a country.

<Method>

Eurostat publishes this as an estimate, not a count: the age at which 50% of a
cohort no longer lives in a household with a parent, derived from the EU Labour
Force Survey. Figures are for both sexes and are the ones Eurostat published on
16 April 2026; nothing here is modelled on top of them.

Eurostat flags a **break in series** in 2021, when the labour force survey was
redefined, for 34 of the 36 territories in the dataset. A reading from 2020 and
one from 2021 are therefore not the same measurement, and no figure in this post
is a difference across that year. The first chart draws only the survey running
now, where every country has the same five readings; the second stops in 2020,
where the older one ends.

A country appears if the older survey covered it at least ten times, the newer
one at least three, and it reported in the last year available. That drops the
United Kingdom, whose series ends in 2019, and Türkiye, whose series ends in
2024.

The first year of the new survey produced some readings that later years walked
back. Portugal's 33.3 in 2021 against 30.1 in 2022 is the largest. They are
Eurostat's published figures and are drawn as published.

`npm run data:leaving-home` rebuilds `src/data/leaving-home.json` from the raw
API response kept in `data/source/`. It refuses to write if any figure quoted in
this post has moved.

</Method>
