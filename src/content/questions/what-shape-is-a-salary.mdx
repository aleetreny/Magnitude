A wage is usually one number. But almost nobody is paid the average of their job, and
the way people spread around it has a shape.

Two families fall out. At the top, a long tail of part-time contracts and
part-years: cleaning, kitchens, teaching, care. At the bottom, the opposite, a
minority pulling away on overtime, night shifts and seniority: painters,
welders, electricians, mechanics.

So the shape is not a symptom of the pay. It is a second fact about a job, and
the one that decides whether the wage on the contract is a promise or an average
of two different lives.

<Method>

The INE publishes five figures per occupation: the mean and the 10th, 25th,
50th, 75th and 90th percentiles of gross annual pay. The curve between them is
reconstructed by monotone (PCHIP) interpolation in log-salary, so it passes
exactly through every published percentile; the build refuses the dataset if any
reconstructed percentile drifts from the published one. Everything outside
p10–p90 is extrapolated rather than measured.

Lean is `log(p90/p50) ÷ log(p50/p10)`, how far the top half reaches against how
far the bottom half does, so 1 is an even shape. The correlation quoted is
Pearson's on log middle wage against lean across all 49 trades; Spearman's is
0.16. A **†** marks estimates the INE flags as resting on 100–500 observations.

The survey covers employees only, so the self-employed are out, and with them
the proprietor sub-groups, domestic work and military occupations. Occupation
names keep the INE's own Spanish wording, since they are official category
names.

</Method>
