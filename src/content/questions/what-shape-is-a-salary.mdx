Take a trade's pay, divide it by its own median, and the money disappears.
What is left is a shape.

Read the wave from the top and the shapes tilt. At one end the mass hangs to
the left of the middle: a long lower tail of part-time contracts and part-years
— cleaning, kitchens, teaching, care. At the other it leans right: a minority
who pull away on overtime, night shifts and seniority — painters, welders,
electricians, mechanics.

So the shape is not a symptom of the pay. It is a second, independent fact
about a job — one that the salary figure on the contract cannot tell you, and
that decides whether the median is a promise or an average of two different
lives.

### Method

The INE publishes five figures per occupation: the mean and the 10th, 25th,
50th, 75th and 90th percentiles of gross annual pay, at the finest level where
a wage distribution exists. The density between them is reconstructed by
monotone (PCHIP) interpolation of the quantile function in log-salary, so it
passes exactly through every published percentile; a check in the build refuses
the dataset if any reconstructed percentile drifts from the published one.

Lean is `log(p90/p50) ÷ log(p50/p10)` — how far the top half reaches against how
far the bottom half does, so 1 means an even shape. The correlation quoted is
Pearson's on log median pay against lean, across all 49 trades; Spearman's rank
correlation is 0.16. Silhouettes are drawn out to 2.6 times each trade's own
median, and beyond p10–p90 the curve is extrapolated rather than measured. A
**†** marks estimates the INE flags as resting on 100–500 observations.
